# KESSEL — design spec

The second game. Operational-level war on a province map: you win by cutting supply
and breaking cohesion, not by grinding stacks. Target lessons are concentration of
force, the culminating point, envelopment, and war aims — the things Risk cannot teach
because Risk has no logistics, no morale and no way to end short of conquest.

This is the target, not a description of what exists. `src/games/kessel` is the
implementation.

## The one-sentence rule

Formations are pushed back by combat and destroyed only when they cannot retreat —
so encirclement, not attrition, is how an army dies.

Everything else exists to make that rule bite.

## Map

Generated by `npm run gen-map` from `data/maps/europe.seeds.json` over a public-domain
Natural Earth coastline. ~135 provinces, average degree ~5, Europe from Iberia to the
Volga. Provinces carry `terrain`, `depot` capacity and `vp`; edges carry shared border
length, which sets attack frontage.

Seeds are hand-placed real cities, so density follows real settlement: the west is
dense and grinding, the east is deep and open. Topology is the game's main tuning
surface — edit the seed file and regenerate rather than patching geometry.

## Formations

| field | meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `strength` | steps, 1–4. Lost slowly, and mostly to encirclement. |
| `cohesion` | 0–100. Readiness. Lost fast, regained in supply. At 0 the formation routs. |
| `type` | `infantry` · `armour` · `recon` |

Strength is what you have. Cohesion is what you can use today. Splitting them is what
makes maneuver something other than faster attrition: a frontal attack spends cohesion
on both sides and moves the line, and a formation that has been pushed twice is still
at full strength but can no longer attack.

- **infantry** — slow, cheap, draws 1 supply, holds ground well, entrenches
- **armour** — fast, expensive, draws 2 supply, high combat, exploits after a
  breakthrough. Draws double, so armoured spearheads culminate first. That is the
  lesson, not a balance knob.
- **recon** — fast, weak in combat, cheap. Exists to sit behind the line and sever
  supply chains. Makes supply an active domain rather than bookkeeping.

## Supply

Depots are supply sources with a capacity of 1–3. Supply traces outward from each
depot through friendly-held provinces that are not in an enemy zone of control, at a
cost of 1 per province and 2 through mountain, marsh or forest. A formation draws from
the cheapest reachable depot that still has capacity.

Degradation is graded, never deletion:

| state | effect |
| --- | --- |
| 3 supplied | full |
| 2 strained | may not attack |
| 1 failing | combat halved, cohesion decays each turn |
| 0 cut off | strength attrition each turn |

Capturing a depot resets chain depth for whoever holds it, which is why depots are
what offensives are aimed at. Encircling a force sends its depth to infinity and the
pocket collapses without a frontal assault.

## Combat

Low variance, so the reviewer can price a decision rather than narrate a dice roll.

Attack value is `Σ strength × cohesion × supply`, defence the same × terrain ×
entrenchment. The ratio drives cohesion loss on both sides — the loser loses more.
When a defender's cohesion reaches zero it retreats one province.

**A formation ordered to retreat that has no friendly-reachable province to retreat
into surrenders.** That single rule is the whole game.

Attack frontage is capped by shared border length, so a province cannot be attacked by
an unlimited stack. Mass still wins, but it has to fit.

## War aims

Total conquest is not the win condition. Each side has a **will** track and an
objective set.

Will falls with formations lost and objectives lost, rises with objectives taken, and
decays slowly every turn regardless — long wars exhaust. Below a threshold a side must
offer terms, and the war ends on the territorial line as it stands, scored against
each side's stated war aims.

So you can win a war you did not conquer, and lose one in which you took ground. This
is the item that roughly halves game length, which matters more than it sounds: play
time is the scarce resource, and the last third of a conquest game is a decided grind.

## Fog

Staged, cheapest-first. The expensive part of fog is hidden *position* — it makes the
bot's belief combinatorial and the reviewer's job ill-posed. The valuable part is
mostly constrained capability, which is nearly free.

1. **Fog of strength.** Province ownership and formation counts are public — front
   lines are known in real war. Strength, cohesion and type are visible only on
   contact or under recon; elsewhere you see the last known value and its age. Belief
   is one scalar per province, so the bot needs no particle filter and the reviewer
   prices against the belief vector.
2. **Order latency.** Formations outside an HQ's command radius execute orders one
   turn late. No hidden state at all, and it produces real friction: you commit to an
   intent and live with it.
3. **Hidden position.** Deferred. Needs determinized search for the bot and
   belief-state pricing for the reviewer, and buys less than either of the above.

## Build order

Each stage is playable before the next starts.

1. Map, formations, IGO-UGO turns, supply, combat, will — full information
2. Fog of strength
3. Order latency and command radius
4. Scenarios with asymmetric war aims
5. Doctrine bots and overnight co-evolution

## How we know it isn't shallow

An invented design has no playtest history, so the open question is whether its
strategy space has a dominant line. `npm run exploit` already answers exactly that
question for Risk: it hill-climbs a parameterised policy space for the strategy a bot
has no answer to and reports the edge as one number. Run it against Kessel from the
first playable build. A large edge found on a small budget means the rules are shallow
and need changing, not that the bot needs work.
